How designers work

Landscape design is a creative problem-solving process. The goal is to organize outdoor space so the natural features of a site and the needs of the people using it are in balance: plants and built elements (walks, patios, fences, structures) arranged so a home or business works well and looks good (adapted from G.M. Pierceall, Residential Landscapes). Landscape designers, landscape architects, interior designers and architects all follow about the same process:

#StepWhat happens
1Research and preparationInterview the client, prepare a base map, do a site inventory and analysis. Done for you: the interview and base plan. You do: the site analysis.
2DesignFunctional diagram, then the general design. You do this. Your presentation is the client meeting where you show it.
3Construction documentsLayout plan, grading plan, planting plan, construction details.
4ImplementationConstruction and installation.
5MaintenancePruning, mowing, irrigation, replacement.
6EvaluationDid the design do what the client needed?

A good design solves the client's problems without creating new ones. A shade tree planted under a power line, or a hedge that blocks the view of traffic from the end of the driveway, both "solve" something and cause a bigger problem. Before you draw anything, account for:

  1. How the client wants to use the space
  2. Budget
  3. Plant selection (see Design reference below)
  4. Soil, rainfall, climate and other site conditions
  5. Existing wildlife
  6. Ordinances, permits, HOA rules and utility setbacks

Design reference

Use this as a checklist while you design. It all comes back to what we covered in lecture: mature plant size, deciduous vs. evergreen leaf habit, and how plants change light, temperature and air movement.

Trees

  • Right tree, right place. Plan for height and spread at maturity, not the size at planting.
  • Scale. Large trees dwarf a small house; they add distinction to a large one.
  • Spacing. 15–20 ft from the side of the house, 12–15 ft from a corner, and large trees no closer than 25 ft to a septic tank, sewer or other utilities.
  • Uses. Visual dividers; a leafy roof over a sitting area; shade that cools the spot below; screens that hide an unsightly area. Evergreens screen year-round and cut down on noise. Two trees can frame a view.
  • Qualities to look for. Strong wood; disease and insect resistance. Lawn trees should have fine texture so light gets through to the turf.
  • Winter sun, summer shade. Put deciduous trees on the southwest side.

Wind protection

  • Windbreaks block and redirect the flow of wind.
  • In our region, winter winds come mostly from the north or northwest and summer winds from the south or southwest. For the Scott site, use the wind directions the client gave you.
  • A windbreak gives its best protection for a distance of about 5–7 times its height downwind.

Shrubs

  • Practical on small lots where there is no room for big trees.
  • Plant close enough together to form a solid wall, but far enough from the house to leave a dead-air space. Still air insulates better than moving air.
  • Rows of shrubs on the northwest side protect the house from cold winter wind as well as direct summer heat.

Espaliers and vines

An espalier is a plant trained to grow flat against a wall or frame.

  • Shades walls and windows in summer and buffers the wall from wind in winter.
  • Deciduous types let sun reach the wall in winter.
  • Vines can be used as ground cover or trained on an arbor (American wisteria, Carolina jessamine).
  • Overhead structures such as arbors and slatted wood shade structures do the same job where there is no room for a tree.

Lettering reminders

  • Keep the pencil point sharp and roll it as you go so the line weight stays even.
  • Press a little harder at the start and end of each stroke.
  • Always draw light guidelines first with a 2H or 4H pencil; vertical strokes touch both lines.
  • Space between lines of lettering should be about half the letter height.

Presenting to your client

On presentation day the class is the Scott family. You get 3–5 minutes to present your design, then the client gets to ask questions.

When you present

  • Put your plan where the client can see it and point to what you are talking about.
  • Go through the client's list. Show where each item is and why you put it there.
  • Explain how your design handles sun, wind, the utility lines and the busy street.
  • Talk about what the family gets ("shade on the patio by late afternoon"), not about drawing symbols.

When you are the client

When you are in the audience, you are the Scotts. Ask the questions a homeowner would ask, for example:

  • Where do the kids play ball, and will the ball end up in the garden?
  • Will we have shade on the patio when we eat dinner in July?
  • Can the people driving by see into our patio?
  • What will the front yard look like in January?
  • Where do the birds come in?
